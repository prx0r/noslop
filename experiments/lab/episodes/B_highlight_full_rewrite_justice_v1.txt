# Justice

Justice is one of those words we use so readily that we risk forgetting what it demands of us. We speak of justice as though it were a destination, a place we might arrive at if only the right laws were passed or the right verdicts delivered. But justice is less a destination than a discipline — a way of attending to the world that requires constant vigilance and the willingness to be uncomfortable.

To seek justice is to acknowledge that something is wrong, deeply wrong. It is to look at suffering and refuse the easy consolation that it is simply the way things are. The Hebrew prophets understood this when they thundered against the powerful who devoured widows' houses. The Buddhist tradition understood it when it taught that compassion must extend beyond the boundaries of tribe and caste. Across cultures and centuries, the impulse toward justice has been the impulse to say: every human being deserves to be seen.

Yet justice is also fraught with paradox. It asks us to be impartial, but it emerges from particular lives — lives shaped by history and grief and belonging. A judge who pretends to have no perspective fails at justice, because justice requires understanding the weight of what is at stake. The courtroom may demand neutrality, but the soul of justice is empathy: the capacity to feel what it means to be the one who has been wronged, and what it means to be the one who must answer for the wrong.

There is also the matter of timing. Justice delayed is justice denied, the legal maxim tells us, and yet some injustices are so deep, so woven into the fabric of institutions and habits, that they resist quick remedy. The work of justice is slow, unglamorous, deeply tedious. It happens in the quiet meetings of committees, in the patient drafting of legislation, in the long conversations between adversaries who refuse to give up on each other. Sometimes it satisfies. Sometimes it exhausts. But it is always necessary.

Perhaps most importantly, justice asks us to reckon with ourselves. It is easy to see injustice in the actions of others and blind to our own complicity. True justice begins with the unsettling recognition that we are all capable of causing harm — because we are finite, distracted, and afraid. The just person is the one who has learned to see failure clearly and to make amends without self-pity.

In the end, justice is a practice. It lives in the space between what the world is and what it ought to be, and it asks us, always, to lean toward the latter.
