**On Love**

There is a paradox at the heart of love: it is the most ordinary of human experiences, and yet it remains, after millennia of poetry and philosophy, essentially inexplicable. We fall in love as though falling were something we chose. We speak of it in the language of gravity, of surrender, of being lost — as though love were a place we wander into, not a decision we make. And perhaps that is closer to the truth than we care to admit. Love is not entirely a decision. It is something that happens to us, and then something we must decide, over and over, to hold.

I have come to believe that love is less a feeling than a practice. The feeling is real — that startling warmth, that irrational attention to another person's voice, the way their absence reshapes the room. But feelings, as every married person and every abandoned lover knows, are unreliable narrators. They surge and retreat with a tide we do not control. What endures is not the feeling but the act: the choosing to remain, the discipline of attention, the willingness to be changed by someone else's reality.

This is what separates love from infatuation. Infatuation is a closed loop — it is about the self, about the pleasure of being desired, about the intoxicating mirror another person holds up. Love, genuine love, is an opening. It demands that we loosen our grip on who we thought we were. It asks us to witness another person not as we wish them to be, but as they are — complicated, contradictory, insufficient, and still worthy of our deepest care.

And love is not only romantic. Some of the most profound love I have witnessed exists between old friends who have weathered decades together, between parents who have buried a child and somehow continued, between strangers who choose kindness when cruelty would be easier. Love takes these forms because it is, at its root, a form of attention — a willingness to see another person clearly and to say, with everything we are: you matter.

The philosopher Simone Weil once wrote that attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity. I think she was describing love. Not the grand gestures, not the declarations, but the quiet, sustained act of noticing — of letting another person's existence matter to us, not because it serves us, but because it is real.

We do not master love. We practice it, imperfectly, until it shapes us into something more generous than we were before.
