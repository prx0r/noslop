Memory behaves like weather — shifting, unreliable, occasionally stunning in its capacity to change the light on an ordinary afternoon. We speak of "having" memories as though they were possessions, filed somewhere behind the eyes, retrievable on command. But anyone who has tried to recall a childhood room knows how poorly this metaphor holds. What returns is a feeling about the room: the way the late sun hit the wooden floor, the smell of something baking, the particular quality of silence that meant your mother was reading in the next room.

This is what memory actually does. It edits. It compresses and distorts, choosing meaning over accuracy. The brain takes liberties the way a storyteller does — smoothing edges, sharpening contrasts. It invents details that never existed but feel truer than what did. A single remembered conversation may contain elements from three different occasions, woven together so seamlessly that we never suspect the collage. And yet — and this is the strange part — the feeling it produces is real. The emotion is authentic, even if the event that generated it has been quietly reconstructed.

We tend to mourn our forgetting, as though each lost memory were a small death. But forgetting collaborates with memory. Experience at full volume would be an undifferentiated flood — every detail equally loud, every moment pressing against every other. Forgetting gives memory its shape, carving significance from the raw stone of experience by allowing most of it to fall away, leaving only what mattered — or what the unconscious mind decided mattered, for reasons we cannot always access.

Memory also changes us over time. The person who remembers has become someone other than the person who lived the event. Each recall is a small act of authorship, shaped by who we have become in the interval. Two siblings can share the same childhood and remember it as though they grew up in different families. They did, in a sense. Memory is a dialogue between the past and the present, and the present always gets the last word.

Perhaps this is what makes memory so deeply human. Its imperfection belongs to consciousness at its deepest level. We are built to remember what matters — and what matters is interpretation all the way down. Memory remakes us, again and again, from the raw and contradictory materials of what we have lived.
