# Justice

Justice is one of those words we use so readily that we risk forgetting what it demands of us. We speak of justice as though it were a destination, a place we might arrive at if only the right laws were passed or the right verdicts delivered. But justice is less a destination than a discipline — a way of attending to the world that requires constant vigilance, humility, and the willingness to be uncomfortable.

To seek justice is to acknowledge that something is wrong, not merely inconvenient. It is to look at suffering and refuse the easy consolation that it is simply the way things are. The Hebrew prophets understood this when they thundered against the powerful who devoured widows' houses. The Buddhist tradition understood it when it taught that compassion must extend beyond the boundaries of tribe and caste. Across cultures and centuries, the impulse toward justice has been the impulse to say: no human being deserves to be treated as invisible.

Yet justice is also fraught with paradox. It asks us to be impartial, but it emerges from particular lives — lives marked by history, grief, and belonging. A judge who pretends to have no perspective is not more just but less, because justice requires understanding the weight of what is at stake. The courtroom may demand neutrality, but the soul of justice is empathy: the capacity to feel, however dimly, what it means to be the one who has been wronged, and what it means to be the one who must answer for the wrong.

There is also the matter of timing. Justice delayed is justice denied, the legal maxim tells us, and yet some injustices are so deep, so woven into the fabric of institutions and habits, that they resist quick remedy. The work of justice is often slow, unglamorous, and deeply tedious. It happens in the quiet meetings of committees, in the patient drafting of legislation, in the long conversations between adversaries who refuse to give up on each other. It is not always dramatic. It is not always satisfying. But it is necessary.

Perhaps most importantly, justice asks us to reckon with ourselves. It is easy to see injustice in the actions of others and blind to our own complicity. True justice begins with the unsettling recognition that we are all capable of causing harm — not because we are evil, but because we are finite, distracted, and afraid. The just person is not the one who has never failed but the one who has learned to see failure clearly and to make amends without self-pity.

In the end, justice is a practice, not a possession. It lives in the space between what the world is and what it ought to be, and it asks us, always, to lean toward the latter.
