Love is not what we think it is. We spend our lives assembling definitions from song lyrics and film endings, from greeting cards and the fumbling words of people who were also guessing. But love, when it arrives, refuses every framework we have prepared for it. It is less a feeling than a discipline, less a destination than a practice performed in ordinary rooms under ordinary light.

We tend to imagine love as an overwhelming force, something that overtakes us. And there is truth in that. The early hours of love carry a reckless electricity, a willingness to dissolve the borders of the self and merge with another consciousness. But this phase, however intoxicating, is the rehearsal, not the performance. What follows is harder and quieter and more worthy of our attention: the slow, deliberate choice to remain present with another human being who will disappoint you, who will fail to read your mind, who will leave the cupboard open and the light on and the metaphors half-finished.

To love someone over time is to watch your own impatience in a mirror. It is to discover, again and again, that the distance between your expectations and another person's reality is not a problem to be solved but a space to be inhabited. The philosopher Erich Fromm called love an art, and like any art, it demands skill, patience, and a willingness to revise. We are not naturally good at it. We are naturally defensive, self-protective, anxious about abandonment. Love asks us to set those instincts down, not permanently, but often enough that something else can grow in the clearing.

There is also the matter of love's grief. Every act of deep affection contains within it the knowledge that it will end — through change, through distance, through the simple and irreversible passage of time. The Japanese poets understood this. Their cherry blossoms were never only about beauty. They were about the beauty that is inseparable from loss. To love well is to hold both the warmth and the ache in the same hand, and to refuse the false comfort of pretending one without the other.

Perhaps the truest thing we can say about love is that it reveals us. Not as we wish to be, but as we are. It strips away the narrative we have constructed about our own patience and kindness and shows us the raw material underneath. This is not cruelty. It is invitation. Love holds up a mirror and says, gently, *look*. And if we are brave enough to look, we find not perfection but the possibility of becoming more fully ourselves, which may be the only transformation worth pursuing.
